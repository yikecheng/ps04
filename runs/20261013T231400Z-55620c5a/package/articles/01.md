# The Agent Moved Past the Diff, Not Past the Gate

*Evidence current through October 13, 2026.*

*Disclosure: AI assisted research and drafting; a human reviewed the evidence.*

The practical buying decision is no longer whether an agent can generate a plausible patch. It is whether a team can safely delegate a larger slice of delivery without surrendering the controls that make software production reliable. Recent launches widen that slice considerably—but they do not establish autonomous delivery.

<!-- claim:01-c8 -->
Taken together, the launches support a specific inference: coding-agent products are moving beyond generation into workflow orchestration across review, security, repository-scale change, and deployment. That expands where teams can delegate, not what they can safely accept without review.

## The workflow surface expanded

<!-- claim:01-c1 -->
In [its Agentic Batch Changes announcement](https://sourcegraph.com/blog/introducing-agentic-batch-changes), Sourcegraph says the system scopes work across indexed repositories, builds a plan, pilots the change in one repository, and then rolls it out. The agent’s unit of work is no longer necessarily one diff in one codebase.

<!-- claim:01-c2 -->
Cursor says [Rollouts](https://cursor.com/blog/rollouts-and-security-reviewer) reads a diff before merge and produces a monitoring plan that a person can edit. This carries agent involvement past implementation and into preparation for production observation.

<!-- claim:01-c3 -->
In the same announcement, Cursor says Security Reviewer examines every pull request in whole-codebase context and reports suspected vulnerabilities with explanations and proposed fixes. This is a vendor description of scope, not evidence of detection accuracy.

<!-- claim:01-c4 -->
OpenAI says in its [code-review demonstration at 0:43–0:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IZXv6a78NDw&t=43s) that initial cloud reviews can run automatically after its connector receives the necessary repository permissions.

<!-- claim:01-c5 -->
A separate [OpenAI CLI demonstration at 1:23–1:30 and 1:56–2:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PUBc2G0Tj5E&t=83s) showed concurrent tasks running across projects and conversation forks isolated in separate worktrees. Concurrency and isolation are useful control mechanics; the demonstration does not measure output quality.

## The gate still matters

Broader reach makes independent acceptance more important, not less. The strongest counter-signal in the evidence packet asks whether apparent success survives after development ends.

<!-- claim:01-c6 -->
[AgentSWE](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13361) evaluates 25 creation, editing, and optimization tasks. Deliveries are frozen and then tested in isolation on held-out cases, separating visible development performance from delivered behavior.

<!-- claim:01-c7 -->
The AgentSWE authors report that Creation runs finishing development at 90 or above averaged only 9.9 on held-out delivery. The result does not prove that every commercial coding workflow fails; it shows why visible completion cannot stand in for independent acceptance.

<!-- claim:01-c9 -->
The defensible inference is that supervised orchestration—not autonomous acceptance—is production-ready. Sourcegraph’s workflow ends with pull requests awaiting human review and merge. Cursor describes paused progressive rollouts and revert pull requests awaiting approval. AgentSWE supplies a reason for those gates: demonstrated success may not survive frozen, held-out evaluation.

The common mature layer is therefore a bounded control plane: explicit plans, isolated execution, inspectable evidence, and approval points. The accepted evidence does not establish that an agent should decide that its own work is correct, merge it, or complete a rollback without human authorization.

## How far the evidence goes

Cursor, Sourcegraph, and OpenAI sell the featured products. Their materials establish that these workflows exist and describe how they are intended to operate; they do not provide controlled measurements of defect-detection precision, false positives, reviewer effort, incident reduction, rollback reliability, coordination failures, or small-team outcomes.

AgentSWE is independent of those vendors, but it is a 25-task preprint focused on agent software. Its representativeness and repeated-run stability remain uncertain, and its authors have an academic interest in demonstrating that the benchmark discriminates. This evidence cannot rank the tools or show that downstream automation improves production outcomes.

## What to do / what to watch

**What to do:** adopt the control mechanisms before expanding authority. Favor editable plans, isolated changes, evidence that reviewers can inspect, and explicit approval for merge and rollback.

**What to watch:** credible measurements of review burden, false positives, escaped defects, incidents, and rollback outcomes. Until those arrive, treat workflow breadth as a capability claim—not proof of autonomous delivery.
