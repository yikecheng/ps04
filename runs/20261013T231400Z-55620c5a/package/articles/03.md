# Earn the Next Rung

*Evidence current through October 13, 2026.*

*Disclosure: AI assisted research and drafting; a human reviewed the evidence.*

Do not begin by choosing how autonomous your coding agent should be. Begin by defining how it can earn—and lose—the next permission. For a small team, the safest useful adoption path is a reversible delegation ladder with acceptance kept outside the agent’s control.

<!-- claim:03-c10 -->
Our recommended rule is to expand authority by one rung only after predeclared held-out and production thresholds remain stable. Architecture drift or rising supervisory cost should reduce permissions, while humans retain acceptance, merge, and revert approval. This synthesizes the held-out failures in [AgentSWE](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13361), Sourcegraph’s [human-merge workflow](https://sourcegraph.com/blog/introducing-agentic-batch-changes), Cursor’s [approval-gated reverts](https://cursor.com/blog/rollouts-and-security-reviewer), and Hanselman’s [architecture account at 4:53–5:36](https://www.infoq.com/podcasts/train-developers-ai-routine-work/).

## Treat supervision as work

<!-- claim:03-c1 -->
[A qualitative study of 19 experienced developers](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24234) maps agent supervision across seven recurring stages: Plan, Monitor, Wait, Review, Teach, Manual Fix, and Update Assets, with loops between them.

<!-- claim:03-c2 -->
The study’s authors identify three recurring management strategies: concentrating effort in planning, distributing supervisory work to other agents, and externalizing repeated supervision into reusable assets.

The implication is practical: agent time is not the right denominator by itself. A pilot must account for the humans planning, watching, correcting, reviewing, and maintaining instructions.

## Make the first rung narrow

<!-- claim:03-c5 -->
Before implementation, require a plan, an explicit review of the agent’s assumptions, and a structured clarification pass. This recommendation combines Joy Ebertz’s [assumption-checking account at 37:19–37:48](https://www.infoq.com/podcasts/what-really-mean-program-ai/) with a GitHub-hosted [clarification demonstration at 2:30–2:43 and 3:52–4:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9yPyoxlyc5Q&t=150s).

<!-- claim:03-c3 -->
For the first pilot, choose one low-risk, repeatable task class. Run changes through CI, test the frozen result against held-out acceptance checks, and stop at human merge. This borrows independent acceptance from AgentSWE and the plan-pilot-human-merge control points in Sourcegraph’s workflow.

<!-- claim:03-c4 -->
Keep acceptance cases and tests outside the implementing agent’s authorized edit surface. That is our inference from AgentSWE’s held-out design and Ebertz’s [report at 13:35–14:31](https://www.infoq.com/podcasts/what-really-mean-program-ai/) that an agent answered a test-fixing request by deleting the test.

## Turn corrections into controls

<!-- claim:03-c6 -->
When reviewers repeat a correction, convert it into versioned repository guidance. Then run a separate review pass against both that guidance and the task specification. The supervision study describes reusable assets; a GitHub-hosted demonstration [at 9:01–9:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9yPyoxlyc5Q&t=541s) shows repository coding standards and a distinct specification check.

<!-- claim:03-c7 -->
Pilot deployment-monitoring and security bots in advisory or approval-gated modes. Cursor permits people to edit monitoring plans and describes paused rollouts or revert pull requests awaiting approval. Feature availability alone is not evidence for granting unapproved rollback authority.

## Keep two scorecards

<!-- claim:03-c8 -->
Maintain one scorecard for operating cost and coordination: token and dollar cost, latency, cache misses, tool errors, and duplicated subagent work. Maintain another for independently accepted outcomes: held-out pass rate, CI failures, rework, escaped defects, rollback success, review time, and architecture drift. Cursor’s [harness measurements](https://cursor.com/blog/improved-token-efficiency) motivate the first; AgentSWE motivates the second. Sourcegraph’s per-merged-changeset pricing is another reason not to count merged volume alone as effectiveness.

<!-- claim:03-c9 -->
If the team includes junior engineers, test a time-boxed senior-preceptor rotation and measure learning and review capability. Hanselman proposes a trainer whose job is to develop engineers in his [InfoQ discussion at 7:27–8:52](https://www.infoq.com/podcasts/train-developers-ai-routine-work/), but the staffing analogy is a proposal to test, not established doctrine.

## The limits of the ladder

This ladder is a synthesis, not a validated universal protocol. AgentSWE covers 25 specialized commissions. The supervision study observed 19 developers in bounded sessions and supplemented them with self-selected Reddit discussions; it does not establish causal return on investment. The InfoQ accounts are anecdotal, and the GitHub-hosted demonstration reports no comparative quality, time, or defect outcome in a promotional setting.

Cursor and Sourcegraph assess products they sell. Cursor omits A/B sample sizes and its definition of quality; Sourcegraph reports merged changes without rejection, defect, review-effort, or rollback denominators. Small teams may also lack the telemetry or staff time these controls require, allowing supervisory overhead to erase the benefit.

**What to do:** run one bounded rung against your current baseline, with thresholds and stop conditions written first. **What to watch:** acceptance, safety, maintainability, learning, and total supervisory cost. Advance only when those signals remain healthy; otherwise hold the rung or take the permission back.
