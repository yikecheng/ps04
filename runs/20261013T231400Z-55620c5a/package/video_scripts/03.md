# Earn the Next Rung

*Estimated runtime: 4½ minutes*

[VISUAL: A five-rung ladder labeled Plan, Implement, Review, Deploy, Recover. Put a human gate between each rung.]

**NARRATION:** A small team should not choose an autonomy level from a feature list. Authority should be earned through reversible trials. Start with planning and bounded implementation, expand only after independent and production outcomes meet thresholds chosen in advance, and keep acceptance, merge, rollout, and rollback control outside the agent.

[VISUAL: Source-title card for *The Work Behind Delegation*. Arrange seven stage cards in a loop.]

<!-- claim:03-c1 -->
**NARRATION:** An independent qualitative study based on 19 experienced developers mapped seven recurring supervisory stages: Plan, Monitor, Wait, Review, Teach, Manual Fix, and Update Assets. Delegation did not remove supervisory work; it changed its shape.

<!-- claim:03-c2 -->
**NARRATION:** The study’s authors identified three recurring strategies: concentrate effort in planning, distribute supervisory work to other agents, and externalize repeated supervision into reusable guidance assets.

[VISUAL: Pre-implementation checklist: Proposed plan; Assumptions; Clarifying questions. Show a human editing each item.]

<!-- claim:03-c5 -->
**NARRATION:** Move the first human checkpoint upstream. Before implementation, require a plan, an explicit assumption review, and a structured clarification pass. Joy Ebertz’s InfoQ account shows that an agent’s hidden assumptions can be materially wrong, while Matt Pocock’s GitHub demonstration illustrates question-driven alignment before building.

<!-- claim:03-c6 -->
**NARRATION:** When the same correction recurs, turn it into versioned repository guidance. Then use a separate review pass to compare the result with both that guidance and the original task specification. Reusable instructions are maintained engineering assets, not a one-time prompt.

[VISUAL: Pilot flow: one low-risk task class → plan → isolated implementation → frozen hidden checks → CI → human review and merge.]

<!-- claim:03-c3 -->
**NARRATION:** Make the first pilot one low-risk, repeatable task class. Use frozen held-out acceptance checks, run the result through CI, and end at human merge. This combines Sourcegraph’s plan-pilot-review control points with AgentSWE’s stricter separation between visible development and held-out delivery.

<!-- claim:03-c4 -->
**NARRATION:** Keep acceptance cases and tests outside the implementing agent’s authorized edit surface. AgentSWE shows why independent evaluation matters, and Ebertz’s deleted-test example shows how an editable test can become a target instead of a constraint.

[VISUAL: Security and deployment bots feed recommendations into an approval inbox. Buttons read Approve, Pause, and Reject; none is preselected.]

<!-- claim:03-c7 -->
**NARRATION:** Pilot security and deployment bots in advisory or approval-gated modes. Cursor describes editable monitoring plans, paused progressive rollouts, and revert pull requests awaiting approval. Feature availability alone is not evidence for granting unapproved rollback authority.

[VISUAL: Two scorecards side by side. Left: Operating cost and coordination. Right: Independently accepted outcomes.]

<!-- claim:03-c8 -->
**NARRATION:** Maintain two scorecards. The operating scorecard should track token and dollar cost, latency, cache misses, tool errors, review time, and duplicated subagent work. The outcome scorecard should track held-out pass rate, CI failures, rework, escaped defects, rollback success, and architecture drift. Merged-change volume belongs to the activity layer; by itself, it is not effectiveness.

[VISUAL: A senior engineer and junior engineer review an agent-produced pull request together. Add a “Time-boxed experiment” label.]

<!-- claim:03-c9 -->
**NARRATION:** If your team includes junior engineers, test a time-boxed senior-preceptor rotation. Scott Hanselman proposes a formal trainer role focused on developing engineers. Measure learning and review capability instead of assuming that staffing analogy is already proven.

[VISUAL: Return to the ladder. Show thresholds beside each rung and a downward arrow labeled “Reduce permissions” beside warning signals.]

<!-- claim:03-c10 -->
**NARRATION:** Expand authority by only one rung after predeclared held-out and production thresholds remain stable. If architecture drift or total supervisory cost rises, reduce permissions. Humans should continue to own acceptance, merge, and revert approval. The point of the ladder is not maximum autonomy; it is the highest authority your evidence can currently justify.

[VISUAL: Limitation card: “Protocol to test—not a universal standard.” Add small labels for 25 tasks, 19 developers, anecdotes, and vendor-reported results.]

**NARRATION:** This ladder is a synthesis to test, not a validated universal protocol. AgentSWE covers 25 specialized commissions. The supervision study observed 19 developers in bounded sessions. The InfoQ material is anecdotal, and the GitHub demonstration reports no comparative quality, time, or defect outcome. Cursor and Sourcegraph sell the featured systems and omit important denominators. Small teams may also find that telemetry and supervision cost erase the benefit.

[VISUAL: Final decision card: “Advance, hold, or step back?” Under it: acceptance, safety, maintainability, learning, total cost.]

**NARRATION:** Compare the pilot with your own baseline. Advance only when acceptance, safety, maintainability, learning, and total supervisory cost meet the thresholds you set before the trial. Otherwise, hold the rung or step back.
